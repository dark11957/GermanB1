### Special emphasis

Chapter 6 and Chapter 7 are particularly important.

The agent must make the following distinctions very clear:

- **dass versus das:** Conjunction versus article or pronoun.
- **weil versus denn:** Meaning and sentence structure.
- **darum / deshalb / deswegen versus weil:** Result versus cause and their effects on word order.
- **ob versus wenn:** Indirect yes/no questions versus conditions.
- **um ... zu versus damit:** Expressing purpose, including the relationship between subjects.
- **obwohl versus trotzdem:** Subordinate-clause conjunction versus main-clause connector.
- **als versus wenn:** Past single events versus repeated events and conditions.

These distinctions must be reinforced throughout subsequent chapters, not taught only once.

**C. Sentence structure**

Hauptsatz, Nebensatz, inversion, subordinate clauses, sentence brackets, verbs in final position, modal verbs, infinitive constructions and connectors.

Explain active/passive transformations, differences between Vorgangspassiv and Zustandspassiv, formation across the common tenses, and when passive voice sounds natural.

# 5. THE EXACT STRUCTURE OF EVERY LESSON

The agent must use one consistent lesson structure.

Each lesson should resemble a professionally authored language textbook.

## Section 1 — Lesson introduction

Include the lesson number, German title, CEFR level, estimated study time, main grammar targets, vocabulary theme and learning objectives.

Use measurable learning objectives.

For example, objectives should state what the learner will be able to explain, understand or produce—not simply that the learner will “learn German grammar.”

## Section 2 — Lesetext oder Dialog

**Every lesson begins with an original German text.**

This is an essential requirement.

The text must naturally introduce the lesson's vocabulary and grammar.

### Text requirements

| Level | Suggested length | Text complexity |
|---|---|---|
| A1 | 100–150 words | Simple dialogues and everyday situations |
| A2 | 150–220 words | Connected descriptions, experiences and conversations |
| B1 | 200–300 words | Richer stories, articles, practical problems and discussions |
| B2 bridge | 250–350 words | More complex articles, opinions and structured discussions |

The text should contain several meaningful occurrences of the target grammatical structures and approximately 15–25 useful target vocabulary items.

**Important writing requirements:**

- Use authentic, natural German.
- Avoid unnatural conversations constructed exclusively to demonstrate grammar.
- Vary the genres: stories, dialogues, emails, interviews, news-style articles, personal experiences and workplace situations.
- Introduce recurring characters occasionally to create continuity.
- Use situations relevant to adult learners.
- Include natural expressions where appropriate.
- Avoid introducing too many unknown grammatical structures simultaneously.
- Ensure every text is original and grammatically correct.

The agent must not create a disconnected collection of example sentences and call it a reading passage.

## Section 3 — Reading comprehension

Follow every reading passage with comprehension activities.

Include questions checking both general understanding and specific details.

Use a mixture of multiple-choice questions, true/false questions, short responses and vocabulary-in-context questions.

Require the learner to understand the content before examining the grammar.

## Section 4 — Wortschatz

Create a vocabulary section directly connected to the reading passage.

Each entry should contain the German word or expression, English meaning, grammatical information where relevant, and an original contextual example.

For nouns, always include the article and plural.

For verbs, include the infinitive and the relevant grammatical information, linking to the full verb reference.

Distinguish between:

- **Active vocabulary:** Words the learner should practise producing independently.
- **Recognition vocabulary:** Words the learner should understand when reading or listening.

Introduce approximately 15–25 active vocabulary items per lesson, with additional recognition vocabulary where justified.

Avoid padding vocabulary lists with obscure or irrelevant words.

## Section 5 — Grammatik verstehen

This section explains the target grammar.

For every grammar rule, provide:

**Purpose:** What the grammatical construction expresses.

**Formation:** How it is constructed.

**Rules:** The essential grammatical principles.

**Examples:** At least three original examples showing different uses.

**Tables:** Conjugation or declension tables when helpful.

**Comparisons:** Distinctions between similar constructions.

**Common mistakes:** Typical errors made by German learners.

**Exceptions:** Important exceptions and irregular patterns.

**Usage:** Whether a form is formal, informal, primarily written or common in conversation.

English should be used for difficult explanations, particularly during the early chapters.

German should remain the primary language of reading passages, examples and practice activities.

As the course progresses, gradually increase the amount of German used in instructions and explanations.

## Section 6 — Übungen: Controlled practice

Each lesson must contain approximately **12–18 individually answerable practice items**, distributed across different exercise types.

Include sentence completion, verb conjugation, correct case selection, sentence reordering, grammatical transformation, error correction and connector selection.

Choose exercises appropriate to the lesson's grammar.

Avoid presenting 15 nearly identical fill-in-the-blank questions.

All objectively answerable activities must have verified solutions.

## Section 7 — Übersetzung

Every lesson must include translation exercises.

Practise both English → German and German → English.

The English → German exercises should progressively require the learner to construct complete sentences using the target grammatical structures.

Provide correct solutions and alternative translations where multiple natural answers are possible.

**Do not treat translation as a mechanical word-for-word exercise.**

## Section 8 — Eigene Sätze schreiben

Require the learner to construct original German sentences.

Provide clear prompts requiring specific grammatical structures.

Progress from short basic sentences in A1 to longer connected statements in B1.

This section must encourage productive use of the language, not copying from the previous examples.

## Section 9 — Schreiben

Every lesson must contain a writing assignment.

Use different types of writing:

Personal messages, emails, short narratives, descriptions, summaries, requests, complaints, opinions and short essays.

Increase the expected writing length progressively.

Provide a checklist covering the communicative task, grammar, vocabulary, organization and appropriate register.

For more substantial writing tasks, include an original model response in the answer section.

## Section 10 — Sprechen und Kommunikation

Every lesson must contain a practical speaking assignment.

Include role-playing situations, personal questions, short presentations or simulated conversations.

Where appropriate, provide two sets of role instructions so the activity can be performed with another person or simulated independently.

Include useful phrases for starting conversations, asking questions, responding, requesting clarification and ending discussions.

At B1, emphasize giving reasons, describing experiences, handling problems, expressing opinions and maintaining a conversation.

## Section 11 — Hörverstehen und Aussprache

Provide an opportunity to hear spoken German.

Use original dialogue scripts that can be narrated or legally usable external audio material.

Include listening comprehension questions and access to the transcript after the attempt.

Browser text-to-speech can be offered as an additional pronunciation aid, but it must not be presented as a substitute for authentic listening practice.

Include pronunciation guidance for difficult sounds, word stress, rhythm and intonation where relevant.

## Section 12 — Wiederholung und Lösungen

End every lesson with:

A short review of previously studied material, a self-assessment checklist and complete solutions for the lesson's exercises.

Solutions must include explanations for important grammatical errors.

**Do not reveal the answers automatically before the learner attempts the exercise.**

In printed versions, place answer keys in a clearly separated solutions section.

# 8. LEARNING, REVISION AND MEMORY SYSTEM

The learner has forgotten previously studied information.

Therefore, simply rereading explanations is not enough.

Implement a systematic review mechanism based on active recall and spaced practice.

## A. Spaced repetition

For important vocabulary and grammar structures, use the following initial review schedule:

| Review | Timing |
|---|---|
| First review | 1 day after learning |
| Second review | 3 days later |
| Third review | 7 days later |
| Fourth review | 14 days later |
| Fifth review | 30 days later |

These are practical starting intervals, not fixed scientific requirements. Allow the schedule to adapt to the learner's performance.

If an item is repeatedly answered incorrectly, bring it back sooner.

If an item is consistently answered correctly, increase the interval.

Prioritize **recalling answers from memory**, rather than simply displaying the same explanation repeatedly.

## B. Cumulative reviews

Every three chapters, create a comprehensive assessment using material from both recent and earlier chapters.

Each assessment should include grammar questions, vocabulary recall, reading comprehension, sentence construction and a short productive task.

Provide results by grammatical topic rather than only one overall percentage.

## C. Initial diagnostic assessment

Before beginning the course, offer a diagnostic assessment lasting approximately 45–60 minutes.

Include around 30–40 grammar and vocabulary questions, short reading comprehension, a writing task and a speaking self-assessment.

It should test foundational structures such as the four cases, past tenses, adjective declension, word order, subordinate clauses, prepositions, relative clauses and Konjunktiv II.

The results should identify topics as:

**Needs revision / Developing / Confident**

The learner should be able to revisit weak lessons directly.

Do not use the results to claim an officially certified CEFR level.

## D. Personal mistake notebook

Create a feature named:

**Meine Fehler — My Mistakes**

Allow the learner to save difficult words, incorrect answers and troublesome grammar structures.

These items should automatically become candidates for future revision.

Add a filter to review only previously incorrect questions.

## E. B1 readiness criteria

The course should end with a meaningful self-assessment.

The learner should demonstrate that they can understand connected B1-level texts, narrate experiences, explain reasons, handle routine problems, write connected messages and maintain conversations on familiar topics.

Use separate rubrics for grammar accuracy, vocabulary, reading, writing, listening and speaking.

The learner's scores should guide the next stage of study, not be represented as a guaranteed telc examination result.
